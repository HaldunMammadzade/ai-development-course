### Positioning

**One sentence.** An AI-native platform that helps people become real software developers.

**Value proposition.** A personal technical mentor that makes you answer, write, fail, understand, and build — then keeps the standard as the work gets harder.

**Audience.** Ambitious beginners and early-career developers who want a job-ready practice, not another video catalog. Secondary audience: career-switchers who will stay for years as the subject matter expands past the frontend path.

**Emotional promise.** You are becoming someone who can do the work, with a mentor who stays when it gets difficult.

**Functional promise.** A sequenced path of lessons, checked practice, projects, review, interviews, and a record of what you can actually build.

**Differentiation.** The product is the loop, not the content library: learn, answer, code, fail, understand, retry, pass, take a harder challenge, remember, build, improve. The mentor is accountable to that loop. Watching a course is not the product.

**What this product is not.**

- A traditional online course catalog.
- A language-locked local school brand.
- A frontend-only bootcamp.
- A chatbot wrapped around documentation.
- A childish learning game.
- An enterprise training suite.
- A generic “AI academy.”

### Brand personality

Intelligent, premium, modern, confident, approachable, ambitious, technical, motivating, and human.

The voice is a precise mentor: short, specific, and calm. It respects the learner’s effort. It does not cheerlead, lecture, or perform “hacker” identity.

Avoid: childish edtech illustration, generic AI-startup language, hacker clichés, crypto aesthetics, excessive neon, generic robots, graduation caps, `</>` marks, circuit boards, brains, and sparkles. Avoid the feeling of corporate enterprise software.

Reference qualities, not identities: the clarity of Linear, the product confidence of Vercel, the craft focus of Cursor, the calm system of Notion, the progression of Brilliant, and the visible path of Duolingo. Do not copy their names, marks, colors, or layouts.

## 2. Brand principles

1. The brand is a platform for becoming a developer. It is not a course title and not a language name.
2. Feature names stay descriptive. Learn, Practice, Projects, Review, Interview, and Career can sit under the brand without becoming the brand.
3. Internal names stay internal. `@platform/*`, database slugs, concept slugs, and project slugs such as `taskflow` are not a rebrand.
4. The symbol must read at 16, 32, 64, 128, and 512 pixels.
5. The name must still make sense when the curriculum leaves HTML, CSS, JavaScript, TypeScript, React, and Next.js.
6. Premium means restraint: one strong idea, durable materials, no costume.
7. A name that already means “developer education,” “AI coding,” or “career platform” to a public audience is a risk, even if it sounds good.
8. No name in this document is approved for UI, domains, or trademarks.

## 3. Naming methodology

Six directions were explored on purpose:

- **A. Abstract premium technology.** Short, ownable, not tied to a subject.
- **B. Learning and growth.** Mastery, ascent, practice.
- **C. Developer and building.** Structure, load, foundation, craft.
- **D. Intelligence and mentorship.** Thought, guidance, judgment.
- **E. Transformation and progression.** Thresholds, sequence, becoming.
- **F. Invented brandable names.** Coined words with no dictionary debt.

Names were rejected when they were generic compounds (`LearnCode`, `CodeAI`, `DevSchool`, `AI Academy`, `*GPT*`) or when they could not outgrow a frontend school.

### Scoring

Twelve names that survived a first qualitative pass were scored 1–10, then revised after a preliminary public-web conflict pass. A name that is already a software, AI, education, or career product received a higher risk score. Risk is the only axis where 10 is bad.

Weighted score, higher is better:

```
(
  brandability      × 1.6
+ memorability      × 1.5
+ global appeal     × 1.4
+ scalability       × 1.3
+ logo potential    × 1.2
+ distinctiveness   × 1.0
+ premium feel      × 1.0
+ pronunciation     × 0.9
+ tagline potential × 0.8
+ developer relevance × 0.7
+ learning relevance  × 0.7
+ AI relevance        × 0.6
+ (11 − risk)         × 1.0
) / 13.7
```

Priority order encoded by the weights: brandability, memorability, global appeal, scalability, logo potential. Technical flavor is intentionally down-weighted. The recommendation is the highest weighted score after the conflict pass, not a preference for the most “technical” sound.

### Research limit

Web search on 8 October 2026 was used only to notice obvious public uses. It did not check WHOIS, trademark registers as a clearance search, app-store availability, or social-handle availability, except where a public page itself showed a product. Anything not opened in that pass is marked unverified.

### Brand architecture

The brand is the platform. The words under it are areas, not products that need their own companies.

**Stylobate**

- Stylobate Learn — the path and the daily lesson
- Stylobate Practice — homework and retries
- Stylobate Projects — TaskFlow-class builds stay project titles inside this area
- Stylobate Review — mentor review of a plan or a milestone
- Stylobate Interview — technical interviews
- Stylobate Career — portfolio, readiness, and the next role

Spoken form: “I’m on Stylobate” means the platform. “I shipped a Stylobate project” means the work. The name does not break when a backend path is added.

**Corbel**

- Corbel Learn, Practice, Projects, Review, Interview, Career
- The metaphor is the mentor holding weight. Product areas read as what the corbel supports. Slightly less natural as a company you “join,” very natural as a system that holds you.

**Noema**

- Noema Learn through Career
- Strongest on Review and Learn, because the name is about understanding. Projects and Interview still work: “show you understand it.”

**Ravel**

- Ravel Learn through Career
- Strongest on Practice and Projects, where work is pulled apart and reassembled. Weaker as a long-term company name because other Ravel software already occupies that sentence.

**Gradia**

- Gradia Learn through Career
- Reads immediately as a learning product, which helps the first page and hurts the premium technology position. “Gradia Interview” sounds like a test prep brand.

## 8. Visual directions

Shared constraints: no robot, cap, `</>`, brain, circuit, or sparkle. The mark has to survive at 16 pixels as a silhouette.

### Stylobate

- **Logo.** A wordmark in a precise grotesque, medium weight, slightly wide. The mark sits to the left: a low horizontal mass.
- **Symbol.** One slab, thicker at the base, with a single small step on the top edge. At 16 px it is a stable rectangle with one notch. It means “platform,” not “temple.”
- **Type.** Neutral grotesque for UI (the current Source Sans 3 can stay). A sharper grotesque for the wordmark. Serif only for long-form essays, not for the logo.
- **Color.** Ink `#1C1915`, stone `#E7E1D6`, a single oxidized-bronze accent `#8C6A45`. Almost no color in the product UI. The accent marks the current step only.
- **Shape.** Horizontal, load-bearing, few radii. Corners are barely rounded, never bubbly.
- **Motion.** The slab settles by a few pixels, then a thin line rises from the step. Duration under 400 ms. No bounce.
- **App icon.** Stone field, ink slab, bronze step. No letter, so it does not depend on a Latin “S.”
- **Favicon.** The slab silhouette alone.

### Corbel

- **Logo.** Wordmark with a tight, architectural italic on the last letter only if testing shows it still reads. Prefer roman.
- **Symbol.** A corbel in profile: wall, then a quarter-step that projects. At 16 px it is an L with a thickened foot.
- **Type.** A sturdy grotesque, slightly condensed.
- **Color.** Graphite `#24272B`, plaster `#F4F1EA`, oxide red `#9C4636` used once per view.
- **Shape.** Right angles and one projecting mass. The projection is the mentor.
- **Motion.** The projection extends, takes a weight (a short line drops onto it), and holds.
- **App icon.** Plaster field, graphite corbel.
- **Favicon.** The projecting step only.

### Noema

- **Logo.** Lowercase wordmark, generous spacing, optical size tuned for “oe.”
- **Symbol.** Two short verticals and a lens-shaped gap between them: attention held on an object. At 16 px it reads as a slot of light.
- **Type.** A contemporary grotesque with a humanist counter, not a geometric tech font.
- **Color.** Near-black `#14181C`, paper `#F7F6F3`, a deep blue-green `#1F4E4A` for the gap only.
- **Shape.** Still, symmetrical, small. The product UI stays quiet so the gap can mean “focus.”
- **Motion.** The gap narrows as if understanding is coming into focus, then rests. Never a sparkle.
- **App icon.** Paper field, one dark lens.
- **Favicon.** The lens alone.

### Ravel

- **Logo.** Wordmark with a single-thread terminal on the R, not a ball of yarn.
- **Symbol.** One line that frays into two and returns to one. At 16 px, a stroke with a small break.
- **Type.** Old-style grotesque, bookish, not a code font.
- **Color.** Walnut `#3A2E28`, ivory `#F3EFE7`, thread gold `#C4A36A`.
- **Shape.** Linear, handmade, slight irregularity. Still geometric enough for a UI.
- **Motion.** The thread pulls taut.

## 10. Brand stories

### Stylobate

A temple is famous for the columns. The stylobate is the platform those columns require. Most programming products sell the columns: a language, a framework, a certificate. This product exists to build the platform — the judgment, the reps, and the finished work under a working developer.

AI is central because a real base is personal. A video cannot tell you that your layout passed and your reasoning did not. The mentor asks, checks, and stays for the retry. The loop is the product: learn, answer, code, fail, understand, retry, pass, take the harder challenge, remember, build, improve. You do not finish Stylobate by watching. You finish a step by making something that holds, and the next step is already a little higher on the same platform.

That is the difference from a course. A course ends. A platform remains under the next discipline — backend, systems, AI engineering, interviews — because the name was never the frontend.

### Corbel

Hard work fails at the joint, not in the middle of an easy lesson. A corbel is the piece that carries load where a wall cannot do it alone. The brand exists for that joint: the hour when the exercise is wrong, the project will not hold, and a person needs something structural beside them.

AI is the corbel, not the author. It holds the explanation, the review, and the next question so the learner can carry the next span alone. The loop is physical: you attempt, the joint fails, you see why, you set it again. Passing is the load transferring back to you. A course can describe the joint. It cannot stand under it.

The name stays honest as the work changes. Interviews, systems, and career reviews are heavier spans on the same wall.

### Noema

The useful moment in learning is not the hint. It is the moment you are holding the right object in mind: what this function is for, why this state bug happens, what the interviewer is actually asking. Noema names that object.

The product exists because people can collect green checks and still not be able to build. AI is central because it can refuse the shortcut: ask again, look at the failed attempt, name the misunderstanding, and only then let you retry. Remembering and building are how you prove the object was real.

A course hands you someone else’s noema. This product makes you form your own, then raise the standard. That is why the same name can sit over a lesson, a project review, and a career conversation.

### Commercial standard

A name has to survive being said:

- “I learned React on [Name].”
- “My AI mentor on [Name] helped me become a developer.”
- “I built this project on [Name].”
- “[Name] just launched AI Engineering.”
- “Start your developer journey with [Name].”
- “Powered by [Name].”

Dictionary beauty does not add points. If the sentence feels unnatural, the score is cut. The cut is a multiplier after the weighted score: `adjusted = weighted × (0.55 + 0.45 × sentence / 10)`. A sentence score of 10 leaves the weighted score unchanged. A sentence score of 6 cuts it by about a fifth.

### Hard filter

RED means a public web result showed a software company, SaaS product, AI startup, developer tool, education platform, coding product, established technology brand, major commercial brand, or a close technology name. RED names do not enter the top 10.

YELLOW means a real use exists and the conflict looks limited by category, geography, or spelling. YELLOW names are listed separately. They do not enter the top 10.

GREEN means this pass did not find a strong commercial conflict of those kinds. GREEN is not clearance.

Research was preliminary public web search, plus WHOIS and a live HTTP check for the finalists. It is not a trademark opinion.

### Status words

**GREEN** means all of the following: no significant direct software, education, AI, or developer conflict found; no obvious Class 9, 41, or 42 trademark conflict found; no strong technology brand with the same name; no obvious pronunciation or spelling collision with a major technology brand; and a domain path that is realistically usable even if the exact .com is taken.

**YELLOW** means usable, with a meaningful adjacent brand, domain, trademark, or search conflict.

**RED** means a strong direct conflict, a strong technology brand, a relevant trademark, or an unacceptable brand or search collision.

A name is not GREEN because a short search missed a software company. None of the ten finalists is GREEN after this pass.

Conflict classes: **DIRECT** (same word, same field: software, AI, developer tools, or education product), **ADJACENT** (same word, different field, or one-letter technology/education collision that a buyer could mix up), **REMOTE** (similar string, different word), **NON-ISSUE** (a username, a dictionary bird page, or a dead filing that does not occupy the brand).
